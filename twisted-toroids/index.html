      What the model shows

      Building it two ways

      The build animation is the gluing rule in motion. First the flat sheet rolls up until its long dashed edges meet, which makes a tube. Then the tube bends until its two solid ends meet, and one end turns by the chosen twist so the paint lines up the way the gluing rule says. That turn is the slide of the sheet's edge in the flat picture. Growing from the shape tells the same story the other way round: the cross-section sweeps once around the loop, turning as it goes, and the tube is the trail it leaves. When it gets back to the start it has turned by exactly the twist, which is why only certain twists let a flat-sided shape land on itself.

      Cutting along the color lines

      Every color boundary is a cut line. After the cut, each strip is a chain of pieces that the twist hands on from one lap to the next, so the number of strips and their lengths come straight from the twist. A strip that goes around q times while turning p times around the core is a (q, p) torus knot: a triangle tube with a ⅔ turn cuts into a right-handed trefoil, and turning the other way gives the left-handed one, its mirror image. The two can't be deformed into each other, so the readouts always say which. The flat strips reproduce the classic Möbius experiments: down the middle of a ½-turn strip gives one strip twice as long, in thirds gives a long strip linked with a smaller Möbius band, and down the middle of a 1½-turn strip gives a trefoil.

      The cut

      The dashed ring marks where the tube was sliced. One end is turned about the tube's own axis before it is glued back, the same half-twist a Möbius band gets.

      Still two-sided

      Turning a circle spins it but never turns it inside out, so the inside of the tube stays inside. The result is still a torus. Only a mirror flip of the cut end would join inside to outside. That surface is the Klein bottle, which can't be built in 3D without passing through itself.

      The paint gets shuffled, not flipped

      Paint applied before the twist can't match at the cut: the red half runs straight into the blue half. Follow the traveler and it rides red for one lap, blue for the next, and only then gets home. The twist permutes the painted pieces at the cut, and a stripe needs as many laps as its piece's cycle is long. That looks like the Möbius band's "go around twice" but it is not the same thing: every stripe on the tube is still two-sided, and cutting along the color lines gives ordinary orientable ribbons. Only the flat strip with an odd number of half turns is a true Möbius band, one-sided with a single edge.

      Why flat-sided tubes show it best

      An end with n flat sides only fits back at steps of 1/n of a turn, its symmetry group Cₙ. The step buttons move by exactly that much. A single step joins all n faces into one face that takes n laps to come home. The oval fits only at half turns, and a round tube fits at any angle, so there only the paint shows the twist. A star or a cross turns like the polygon with the same number of points or arms. The rectangle and diamond fit at half turns, like the oval, and the gear steps tooth by tooth in eighths. The egg and the heart have no turning symmetry at all, so they only fit back after whole turns.

      Do whole turns undo themselves?

      With 2 or 4 full turns the paint lines up again and every stripe comes home in one lap. But each stripe now winds around the tube 2 or 4 times before it closes, and that count is its linking number with the tube's core circle. No motion of the torus in space can change it, so the torus is not back to normal. The belt trick is different: a belt's turning only matters up to 720°, because its free end can swing around. A closed stripe has no free end, so it keeps every turn.

      The antipodal rule

      On a circle, the antipodal map x ↦ −x is the same as a half-turn, a rotation, so it keeps orientation and the torus stays two-sided. On a sphere the two come apart. A half-turn about an axis is still a rotation and keeps orientation. The antipodal map x ↦ −x is not a rotation: it reverses orientation. Gluing each point of the sphere to its antipode gives the projective plane RP², which is one-sided.

      Reading the numbers

      A turn of p/q of a full rotation sends a line home after q laps. With n painted pieces shifted by s places at the cut, the paint forms gcd(s, n) separate stripes.
